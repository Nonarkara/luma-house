# The journey, made walkable — 2026-10-29

The journey the studio is for, said out loud:

1. Come in and be invited to think of it as a napkin.
2. Draw a rectangle with your finger and get a room.
3. Take a wall out, or the whole thing; or pull a corner and make it bigger
   or smaller, the way you resize a rectangle in PowerPoint.
4. Drag a window over and put it on a wall, and it cuts an opening.
5. Trim, like AutoCAD: two intersecting rectangles, and you remove the part
   you want gone.
6. Look at the plan and the 3D together, and place your furniture and your
   openings to make an interior.

One pencil, several meanings. The vocabulary was there — a line is a wall, a
tick is a door or a window, a box is a room — but the **editing** half of the
language was missing. You could draw and you could nudge; you could not take
a wall out without taking the room with it, and you could not put a window
where your finger was.

## What was missing

| step | state before | why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · the napkin | there | the welcome gate invites it |
| 2 · a room from a rectangle | there | `applyNapkinStroke` |
| 3 · take a wall out | **missing** | no eraser of any kind; a room could only be deleted whole, through the inspector |
| 3 · pull a corner | there | `resizeRoom` and the eight handles |
| 4 · drag a window onto a wall | **missing** | the Window and Door tools wanted a *click* on a wall; there was no way to carry one to it |
| 5 · trim | **missing** | two overlapping rooms could be *detected* — the plan flagged them as overlaps — and then you resized by hand until they stopped |
| 6 · the plan and the 3D together | **apart** | two tabs, and the 3D's furniture control was a `<select>` dropdown |
| 6 · place furniture and openings | **awkward** | the catalogue added a piece wherever it found clear floor; there was no putting it where you wanted it |

## What was built

### The eraser (`E`)

One tool, and it takes the topmost thing under the cursor, in the order the
drawing is drawn: **furniture → an opening → an unfinished pencil wall → a
room.** A click removes it and says which one. Removing a room takes with it
the furniture that stood in it and the openings whose wall went with it — an
opening on a wall shared with a neighbour stays, because the neighbour still
has that wall.

### The trim (`C`)

AutoCAD's trim, in a model whose rooms are rectangles. Click a room that
crosses another and it is cut back along the other room's wall, so the
crossing is gone.

* **The cut is the smallest one that clears the crossing.** A room is a
  rectangle and a cut is a straight line, so where more than one wall would
  do, the trim takes the one that costs the least floor. Two rooms meeting
  at a corner can be cleared either way; the trim takes the smaller bite and
  says which wall it followed.
* **A room that lies entirely inside another is removed.** That is the wall
  that came out in another way.
* **A cut that would leave a sliver is refused, not forced.** "Cutting there
  would leave a room too small to use" — and nothing changes.

The cut is **shown before it is made**: the band it would take is hatched, the
room that would be left is outlined, and a badge says how much floor goes and
which wall it follows.

### The warning that now carries the tool

The studio already says when two rooms overlap — *"Rooms overlap — floor area
is double-counted and climate analysis is unreliable"* — and then left the
reader to find a tool that could fix it. That warning now carries **"Trim the
overlap"**: one tap cuts the room that was drawn over another back to the
wall it crossed, and the warning goes with the overlap. A warning that can be
acted on is a decision; a warning that cannot is a complaint.

### Carrying a piece in

Press the Window or the Door in the palette and drag it onto a wall: the
opening follows the pointer, the plan says where it would land, and letting go
cuts it. Press a piece in the furniture catalogue and drag it onto the plan:
it lands where you let go, at its real size, or at the nearest clear floor in
that room, and it is never shrunk to make it fit.

All of it is built on pointer events, not HTML drag-and-drop, because the
studio is used with a finger.

### The plan and the 3D together

On a sheet wide enough for two — 1100 px and up — the plan can stand the 3D
beside it. One selection is shared: the piece you pick in one is picked in the
other. Below 1100 px the offer is not made, because the plan keeps the whole
width and the 3D is never built at all: building it would cost a megabyte of
code and a second of waiting for a view a phone cannot show.

## The honest boundary

**A room is a rectangle, so a wall is not a thing you can erase off it.**
Erasing one face of a closed room would need walls as entities of their own —
a model change that would touch the sun model, the 3D, the bill of quantities,
every opening and the share format. That is not a change to slip into a
session. So the eraser removes what the model has: furniture, an opening, an
unfinished pencil wall, a whole room — and the **trim** is how you take a
wall out of a finished room, by cutting the room back to it.

## The gates

* `src/canvas/editing.ts` is pure geometry — what is under the cursor, what
  a cut would take, what a room loses — and it has **29 unit tests** that
  name each case: the furniture before the room, the opening before the
  wall, the smallest cut, the room inside a room, the refused sliver, the
  opening that survives its neighbour.
* `e2e/cutting.spec.ts` drives the real interface: the eraser removes a room
  and an opening, undo puts them back, the trim cuts a crossing room back to
  the wall and refuses a sliver, a dragged window lands on the wall it was
  dropped on, and a window dropped on empty paper is not placed and says why.
* `e2e/split.spec.ts` owns the split: the 3D stands beside the plan on a wide
  sheet and the plan still works there; the narrow sheet keeps the whole plan
  and never builds the 3D.

## Audit of the hand-off — what the next session found

The previous session ended mid-work with this change uncommitted. It passed
`tsc`, lint (one error) and the unit suite, and still carried six defects.
Every one was found by reading the diff or by driving the real page.

| 1 | **The split was not side by side.** `studio.css` loads after `styles.css` and its plan-frame grid out-specified the split rule, so the 3D stacked *under* a plan squeezed to 263 px of an 804 px frame. | Measured the rendered rectangles: the 3D sat at y 438, below the plan. | The split is stated in `studio.css` at the frame's own specificity. At 1440 × 900 the plan and the 3D are each 536 × 643 px, the room key keeps its column. |

| 3 | **A trim left the floor's contents standing in the void.** Furniture in the cut band and openings on a cut-off wall stayed in the plan: floating in the 3D, still counted in the bill. | Reading `trimAt`: it cut the room and touched nothing else. | `settleAfterCut` removes the furniture whose centre is now in no room and the openings that lost their wall, and the toast says how many.
